\begin{abstract}
Root cause analysis (RCA) in machining aims to explain why a milling operation departs from healthy behaviour, for example because of a loose clamp, a worn tool, an excessive depth of cut or an unsuitable spindle speed, and it is a prerequisite for autonomous, zero-defect production.
Real cuts with known causes are scarce and costly to produce, so simulation is the natural source of labelled data, and models trained on simulated cuts have already been transferred to real machines for single fault families such as chatter.
These studies, however, build the diagnostic models and the test faults from the same simulator, treat one fault family on one machine, and do not measure how large the gap to real signals is or which part of it matters.
We present a provenance-tracked regenerative-milling simulator whose healthy baseline is modelled from real multi-sensor spectra, and an evaluation framework that separates the healthy gap, the fault-signature gap and the circularity of the diagnostic procedure, applied to six causal RCA algorithms and to real-data transfer baselines.
Experiments show that accuracy that is near perfect inside the generator falls from 0.80--0.98 to 0.35--0.71 when the generator's parameters are shifted, that the causal graph contributes nothing in this benchmark, and that no simulated fault reproduces the signature of a real defective tool.
The simulator, the benchmark and the protocol are open source at \url{https://github.com/danghaidang04/RCA-for-Chatter-Simulation}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
Milling is the workhorse of discrete manufacturing, and its quality depends on a small set of physical conditions that are easy to disturb: how stiffly the workpiece is clamped, how far the tool overhangs, how worn the cutting edge is, and whether the programmed depth of cut and spindle speed lie inside the stable region of the machine--tool system.
\emph{Root cause analysis} (RCA) for milling takes the signals that a machine already produces (accelerometers on the spindle, a microphone, spindle power and controller values, sometimes a force dynamometer) and returns a ranked list of the physical causes that explain an observed deviation from healthy cutting.
The remedy depends on the cause: a loose fixture is re-clamped, a worn tool is replaced, an excessive depth is re-programmed, and a speed that falls into a lobe pocket is moved.
Detecting that something is wrong is therefore not enough; autonomous and zero-defect production, and the reduction of scrap and unplanned downtime, require the machine to say \emph{why}.

\paragraph{Why the problem is hard.}
The internal quantities that define the causes (stiffness, damping, cutting-force coefficients, tool wear) cannot be measured during cutting, and the same symptom, for instance a louder and more broadband signal, can arise from several of them.
Real labelled data are the obvious route to learning the mapping from signals to causes, but they are expensive and often impossible to collect in numbers: causes such as a deliberately loosened clamp, an overloaded tool or a chipped flute damage parts, tools and sometimes the machine, and each requires a controlled cutting campaign on every machine of interest.
Public machining datasets therefore record sensor streams together with, at best, expert labels of normal or abnormal operation \citep{kim2026msm}, or a single measured quantity such as flank wear \citep{denkena2023luh}, but not several known root causes.

\paragraph{What has been done.}
Four lines of work address parts of the problem.
\emph{Physics-based models} predict the stability of milling from the tool-tip transfer function and the cutting-force coefficients \citep{altintas2020}; they say whether a cut will chatter, but they need quantities that are rarely known for a given machine and tool, and they do not infer causes from measured signals.
\emph{Data-driven monitoring} learns chatter or tool-wear detectors from measurements and, to cope with changes of machine and condition, uses transfer learning, domain-adversarial training and meta-learning \citep{yesilli2022tlchatter,wang2020datwear,li2023metadg}, sometimes combined with physics or digital twins \citep{li2025physguided,yang2022hybridwear,han2025dtmech}; these detectors target one fault family and rely on real labels from the source machines.
\emph{Simulation-to-real learning} trains on simulated cuts and applies the model to a real machine: random forests trained on process simulations detect chatter \citep{alberts2024sim2real} and tool-related faults \citep{ebrahimi2023smartfaults}, and domain randomisation, adversarial refinement and simulation-based inference are general tools for the same purpose \citep{tobin2017,shrivastava2017,cranmer2020}.
\emph{Causal root cause analysis} ranks the nodes of a causal graph by how much their mechanism changed between healthy and anomalous data \citep{ikram2022rcd,ikram2025rcg,pham2024baro,orchard2025,fang2025simplerca,lee2026brcd}; it was developed for software systems and has not, to our knowledge, been applied to machining signals with known causes.

\paragraph{The remaining challenge.}
Simulation-to-real studies in machining report that a model works on a real machine, but they do not tell the reader how much of the accuracy is due to the simulator itself.
Three parts of the simulation gap are not separated in the literature.
First, the \emph{healthy baseline} of a simulator may differ from real signals, so that a classifier learns to recognise the simulator.
Second, the simulated \emph{fault signatures} may differ from real ones, so that learning the former does not diagnose the latter.
Third, the \emph{diagnostic procedure} may exploit the generator: when the nodes of a causal graph are estimated by regressors trained on labels produced by the very simulator that generates the test faults, a high accuracy is guaranteed by construction (Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}).
Without separating the three, a high simulated accuracy cannot be interpreted, and a simulator whose parameters have no source can silently decide the outcome of a benchmark.

\paragraph{Our solution.}
We build a pipeline in which every ingredient is accountable to real data (Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}).
The \emph{simulator} is a regenerative milling model whose parameters carry a provenance tier (fixed, ranged, learned or assumed), so that unsupported assumptions are visible.
The \emph{healthy baseline} is modelled from real accelerometer and microphone spectra rather than simulated, so that the healthy gap can be measured directly with a classifier two-sample test; the simulator contributes only the \emph{difference} between a faulty and a healthy run, scaled to real effect sizes.
The \emph{RCA stage} runs six causal algorithms on a 14-node graph and measures the circularity of the benchmark by shifting the generator's parameters at test time and by ablating the graph and the way the nodes are built.
Finally, the simulated and real signals are compared on real data: a healthy baseline, a real defective tool, and a public multi-machine wear dataset on which simulation is pitted against domain-adaptation and one-feature baselines.
The insight is that the question ``does simulation support diagnosis?'' has to be decomposed before it can be answered, and that each component can be measured with data that are publicly available today.

\paragraph{Findings.}
Accuracy near one inside the generator is not evidence of diagnostic ability: it drops sharply under a moderate shift of the generator, does not depend on the causal graph, and is close to chance when nodes are built without simulator labels.
The simulated healthy baseline and the signature of a real defective tool remain distinguishable from real data, and the nominal simulator turns out to lie above its own stability limit for every real cutting condition.
On a public wear dataset, simulation-only transfer to unseen machines is machine-dependent and does not beat a one-feature baseline on two of three machines, and fine-tuning from simulation is no better than training from scratch on this task.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A measurement of benchmark circularity.} We show for six causal RCA algorithms that accuracy depends on how nodes are constructed and not on the causal graph, and quantify its collapse under generator shift (Section~\ref{sec:main}).
\item \textbf{A component-wise measurement of the simulation gap with negative results.} A real-data healthy baseline with a classifier two-sample test, a comparison with a real defective tool including a chipped-flute hypothesis, and a stability analysis of the simulator (Section~\ref{sec:analysis}).
\item \textbf{Evidence on when simulation helps on real data}, with domain-adaptation, one-feature and real-only baselines on a public three-machine dataset (Sections~\ref{sec:main} and~\ref{sec:ablation}).
\item \textbf{An open pipeline}: a provenance-tracked simulator with FRF import, a precise account of the RCA algorithms as implemented and of their deviations from the originals, and an evaluation protocol for real multi-cause data (Sections~\ref{sec:method} and~\ref{sec:future}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
We organise the literature by the part of the problem it addresses and finish with a summary of what each line leaves open (Table~\ref{tab:gap}).

\subsection{Physics-based modelling of milling dynamics and chatter}
Regenerative chatter is a self-excited vibration caused by the delay between the vibration marks left by successive teeth.
Its stability can be predicted analytically with the zero-order approximation of the stability lobes, in the frequency domain with multi-frequency and semi-discretisation methods, and in the time domain with delay-differential-equation simulation \citep{altintas2020}.
These models take the tool-tip transfer function and the cutting-force coefficients as inputs and return a stability boundary or a simulated vibration.
They are the right foundation for a simulator of faulty cuts because the effect of a stiffness, damping, force or speed change on the signal follows from the model.
Their limitation for diagnosis is the inverse direction: they predict the signal from known parameters but do not infer the parameters (the causes) from measured signals, and the parameters of a given tool, holder and machine are rarely known precisely.

\subsection{Data-driven monitoring and transfer for tool condition}
Learning-based chatter detectors and tool-wear estimators are trained on measured vibration, sound, force or power signals.
Because their accuracy falls when the machine, tool or cutting conditions change, transfer learning for chatter detection \citep{yesilli2022tlchatter}, domain-adversarial transfer for generalised tool-wear prediction \citep{wang2020datwear} and semi-supervised multi-source meta domain generalisation across cutting conditions \citep{li2023metadg} have been proposed.
Physics-guided deep learning \citep{li2025physguided}, hybrid online data--model wear detection \citep{yang2022hybridwear}, digital twins with a dynamics core \citep{han2025dtmech} and single-sensor wear prediction \citep{hirsch2025singlesensor} add structure or reduce the sensing effort; systematic reviews of physics-informed learning for prognostics and health management and of simulation-to-real transfer in digital twins are given by \citet{braun2026pinnphm} and \citet{ma2025dtpdm}.
These works address one fault family (chatter or wear), rely on real labelled data from the source domains, and do not rank several root causes.
We use their ideas (domain-adversarial training, correlation alignment) as baselines on public wear data.

\subsection{Simulation-to-real learning in machining}
\citet{alberts2024sim2real} train a random forest on more than 140\,000 simulated chatter cases and fine-tune it with 10\% of 1\,600 real instances from one custom three-axis machine, reaching 86.1\% accuracy on a chatter-versus-stable decision; they state limitations related to the simulation-to-real difference.
\citet{ebrahimi2023smartfaults} train random forests on analytical process-simulation data to monitor tool-related faults in milling and report 94\% accuracy, and 93\% on actual measurements (from the abstract; full text not read).
These studies establish that simulation-to-real training is feasible for one fault family on one machine.
They do not decompose the simulation gap, test how much of the accuracy comes from sharing the generator between training and testing, or consider several root causes and several machines.
Generic tools for narrowing a simulation gap include calibration with a discrepancy term \citep{kennedy2001}, domain randomisation \citep{tobin2017}, adversarial refinement of simulated data \citep{shrivastava2017} and simulation-based inference \citep{cranmer2020}; we use a classifier two-sample test \citep{lopezpaz2017c2st} as a diagnostic of the gap, not as a training signal.

\subsection{Causal root cause analysis}
Causal RCA formulates a failure as an intervention on one node of a causal graph and ranks nodes by the change of their mechanism between a healthy dataset and a few anomalous samples.
RCD \citep{ikram2022rcd} tests the independence of a failure indicator from each metric under conditioning, RCG \citep{ikram2025rcg} ranks candidates by conditional mutual information given the parents in a partial causal structure, BARO \citep{pham2024baro} combines Bayesian change-point detection with a robust deviation score, SimpleRCA \citep{fang2025simplerca} compares the maximum of the anomalous data with the 95th percentile of the normal data, and Smooth Traversal \citep{orchard2025} traverses the ancestors of a trigger node using outlier scores.
BRCD \citep{lee2026brcd} computes a Bayesian posterior over root causes from a CPDAG learned before the failure, with consistency guarantees, and compares ten methods on synthetic and microservice benchmarks; its authors observe that the methods based on marginal deviation (BARO, SimpleRCA) are competitive on two of the systems but do not generalise.
These methods were developed and evaluated for software systems.
Applying them to machining requires data with known causes, which is the role of simulation in this work; the algorithms in our code base are simplified re-implementations on a known graph (Section~\ref{sec:rca}).

\subsection{Datasets}
The MSM dataset \citep{kim2026msm} provides synchronised accelerometer, microphone, current and controller data from three machines with expert labels (normal, abnormal, defective tool), and the LUH dataset \citep{denkena2023luh} provides force and machine signals of three machines with three tools each and measured flank wear.
Neither records several known root causes, which is why we use them for the healthy baseline, the signature of one real cause, and the study of transfer between machines.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}
\subsection{Problem formulation and overview}\label{sec:overview}
We consider a milling cut described by its condition $c=(n,a_e,a_p,f_t,\text{direction})$ (spindle speed, radial and axial depth, feed per tooth, up or down milling) on a machine--tool system $\theta$ (modal dynamics, force coefficients, sensor constants).
A cut yields sensor signals $s$ (two accelerometer channels and a microphone, windowed to 0.3~s).
A root cause is the change of one physical factor $z_j$ among five (clamp stiffness, damping, tool wear, radial depth, spindle speed) relative to the healthy value.
Given a healthy dataset $D_\text{obs}$ and $m$ anomalous windows $D_\text{int}$ produced by an unknown cause, the \emph{RCA task} is to return a ranking of the nodes of a causal graph $G$ in which the true cause is as high as possible.
Because real $(D_\text{int},\text{cause})$ pairs are scarce, the data for developing and testing RCA come from a simulator that must be accountable to real data.
Figure~\ref{fig:architecture} gives the architecture; Section~\ref{sec:input} describes the inputs, Section~\ref{sec:sim} the simulation and Section~\ref{sec:rca} the RCA stage.

\subsection{Input}\label{sec:input}
\paragraph{Motivation.} The result of a simulation-based benchmark depends on every number that enters the simulator; the inputs therefore have to be explicit, and each must say where it comes from.
\paragraph{Design.} The input consists of five groups (Table~\ref{tab:inputs}).
(i) The \emph{cutting condition} is read from the label files of the real data (spindle speed, feed, radial depth, direction) and verified against the controller values (ratio 1.000).
(ii) The \emph{tool and machine description} fixes the geometry (2SE0250IX150A: two flutes, 6.35~mm, flute length 38.1~mm, helix $30^\circ$) and the modal dynamics of the tool tip; for a new machine the dynamics are read from a hammer-test transfer function by the FRF importer (Section~\ref{sec:frf}).
(iii) The \emph{force and sensor constants} (cutting-force coefficients, imbalance, gains and noise floors) are the parameters that had no usable source.
(iv) The \emph{fault specification} defines five faults and their severity ranges.
(v) The \emph{real healthy cuts} supply the spectra from which the healthy baseline is built.
Every parameter is registered with a tier: \emph{fixed} (a verified source gives the value for this exact tool and context), \emph{range} (a source gives a range), \emph{learned} (only data constrain it) or \emph{assumed} (neither), with its context and verification status (Figure~\ref{fig:prov}, Appendix~\ref{app:registry}).
\paragraph{Advantage.} The registry makes unsupported assumptions visible to the reader and prevents a literature value from being reused outside the context in which it was measured.

\subsection{Simulation}\label{sec:sim}
\subsubsection{Structural dynamics and cutting forces}
\paragraph{Motivation.} Regenerative chatter arises from the delayed feedback between the vibration marks left by one tooth and the chip thickness of the next; a time-domain model with the correct delay reproduces both stable forced vibration and saturated chatter, and the effect of a stiffness, damping, force or speed change.
\paragraph{Design.} Each tool-tip axis $q\in\{x,y\}$ is a sum of modes with transfer function
\begin{equation}
H_q(s)=\sum_{r}\frac{\beta_r s+\alpha_r}{s^2+2\zeta_r\omega_r s+\omega_r^2},\qquad \beta_r=2\sigma_r,\;\;\alpha_r=2(\zeta_r\omega_r\sigma_r-\omega_{d,r}\nu_r),
\end{equation}
(residues $\sigma_r,\nu_r$; the nominal tool uses one mode per axis with $H=1/[m(s^2+2\zeta\omega s+\omega^2)]$, $m=k/\omega^2$). Each mode is a two-state system $[x_r,\dot x_r]$ discretised exactly with a zero-order hold, $(A_d,B_d)$ read from the matrix exponential of $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}A\,\Delta t & B\,\Delta t\\ 0&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$, at $\Delta t=1/25\,600$~s.
Tooth $k$ of a flute with $N$ teeth is divided into $N_z{=}8$ axial slices of height $dz=a_p/N_z$; the angle of slice $q$ at time $t$ is
\begin{equation}
\phi_{k,q}(t)=\Omega t+\frac{2\pi k}{N}-z_q\frac{2\tan\beta}{D},\qquad z_q=(q+\tfrac12)dz,
\end{equation}
where $\Omega$ is the spindle angular speed, $\beta$ the helix angle and $D$ the tool diameter. A slice cuts only while $\phi_{k,q}\in[\phi_{st},\phi_{ex})$, with span $\arccos(1-2a_e/D)$ (up milling: $[0,\text{span})$; down milling: $[\pi-\text{span},\pi)$). Its chip thickness depends on the current and delayed displacement,
\begin{equation}
h_{k,q}=\Big[f_t\sin\phi_{k,q}+\big(x(t)-x(t-T)\big)\sin\phi_{k,q}+\big(y(t)-y(t-T)\big)\cos\phi_{k,q}+\nu_k(t)\Big]_{0}^{h_{\max}},\quad T=\frac{60}{N\,n},
\end{equation}
with $n$ the spindle speed in rpm, $f_t$ the feed per tooth (offset by $\pm$ the runout for the first two teeth), a fractional delay $T/\Delta t$ implemented by linear interpolation, tooth-wise roughness noise $\nu_k\sim\mathcal N(0,0.02\,\mu\text{m})$, and $h_{\max}=0.3$~mm. The elemental forces are
\begin{align}
dF_t&=(K_t h+K_{te})\,dz,\qquad dF_r=(K_rK_t h+K_{re})\,dz,\\
F_x&\mathrel{+}=-dF_t\cos\phi-dF_r\sin\phi,\qquad F_y\mathrel{+}=dF_t\sin\phi-dF_r\cos\phi,
\end{align}
and spindle imbalance $U$ adds $U\Omega^2[\cos\Omega t,\sin\Omega t]$.
\paragraph{Advantage and check.} The core runs in numba (13 times faster than the Python reference). With helix and edge forces off it reproduces the pure-Python straight-flute reference to $7\times10^{-16}$, and eight slices are converged (Section~\ref{sec:lobes}).

\subsubsection{Stability lobes}
For comparison the zero-order approximation \citep{altintas2020} gives, for each chatter frequency $\omega_c$, the eigenvalue $\Lambda=-\frac{1}{2a_0}\big(a_1-\sqrt{a_1^2-4a_0}\big)$ with $a_0=H_xH_y(\alpha_{xx}\alpha_{yy}-\alpha_{xy}\alpha_{yx})$ and $a_1=\alpha_{xx}H_x+\alpha_{yy}H_y$, the limit depth $a_{\lim}=-\frac{2\pi\Lambda_R}{NK_t}(1+\kappa^2)$, $\kappa=\Lambda_I/\Lambda_R$, and the spindle speeds $n=60/(N T_k)$, $T_k=(\pi-2\psi+2k\pi)/\omega_c$, $\psi=\arctan(\Lambda_I/\Lambda_R)$. The lobes provide an independent check of the time-domain model and a test of whether a parameter set operates in the stable region.

\subsubsection{Sensor chain}
\paragraph{Motivation.} Real RCA data are sensor signals, not displacements; the simulated displacement has to pass through the same kind of measurement chain.
\paragraph{Design.} Four linear time-invariant blocks implemented in \texttt{scipy.signal}: (1) a relative tool--work displacement sensor (low-pass at 8~kHz, noise 0.05~$\mu$m); (2) an accelerometer, $a=\ddot q/g$ band-passed to 5~Hz--10~kHz with a gain to the sensor location and IEPE noise; (3) a microphone, $p=\frac{\rho_0A}{4\pi r}a_\text{res}+c_\text{cut}\dot F_x$ delayed by $r/c$, band-passed to 20~Hz--10~kHz, plus floor noise; and (4) a force dynamometer, $-F$ through a second-order system ($f_n{=}2$~kHz, $\zeta{=}0.03$) plus drift and noise (Figure~\ref{fig:waveforms}). Simulation windows of 0.6~s are generated and the last 0.3~s analysed; signals are resampled with a polyphase filter to the sampling rates of the real data so that identical features can be computed.

\subsubsection{Healthy baseline from real spectra}\label{sec:base}
\paragraph{Motivation.} Fitting the simulator's absolute signal levels directly to real windows did not converge (Section~\ref{sec:neg}), and Section~\ref{sec:regime} explains why the nominal simulator cannot supply a realistic healthy signal; the data constrain the \emph{healthy signal} far better than the physics constants.
\paragraph{Design.} For each 0.3~s window we compute the power spectral density of two accelerometer channels and the microphone and summarise it by a piecewise-linear envelope over log-frequency (18 knots, band-averaged linear power so that energy is preserved) plus the rms of the first eight harmonics of the spindle frequency. Envelope parameters are regressed on the cutting conditions (kernel ridge on $\log n$, $\log a_e$, $\log f$, direction); out-of-fold residuals are split by one-way variance analysis into between-cut and within-cut parts on twelve principal directions. Windows are synthesised by shaping complex Gaussian spectra with the envelope and adding harmonics with random phases (real spectra in Figure~\ref{fig:psd}).
\paragraph{Advantage.} The healthy part of a simulated window is real-data-driven, so any remaining difference to real data (Section~\ref{sec:healthy}) is attributable to the baseline model, not to the physics.

\includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/real_psd_examples.png}
\caption{Power spectral density of four real windows (accelerometer left, microphone right): normal and abnormal cuts at different spindle speeds.}\label{fig:psd}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Fault injection with effect-size gain}\label{sec:fault}
\paragraph{Motivation.} A simulator's absolute fault amplitude is unknown, but the \emph{direction} of a fault's effect on the signal is physically constrained.
\paragraph{Design.} For each window and fault we run the simulator twice with the same condition and noise seed and add $g\,(s_\text{fault}-s_\text{healthy})$ to the baseline, with separate $g_\text{acc},g_\text{mic}$. Faults and ranges (assumed): stiffness $\times[0.4,0.9]$ (natural frequency $\propto\sqrt{k}$); damping $\times[0.3,0.8]$; tool wear $K_t\times[1.1,1.6]$ with $K_{te},K_{re}\times(1+3(s-1))$; radial depth $\times[1.5,4]$; spindle speed $\pm[3,15]\%$. Matching feature distributions did not identify the gains (flat objective), so we set them by effect size: the mean shift of $\log_{10}$ acceleration rms and microphone level equals the real abnormal--normal difference ($+0.375$ and $+4.86$~dB), giving $g_\text{acc}=7.7\times10^{-6}$ and $g_\text{mic}=0.0116$.
\paragraph{Limit.} Real abnormal windows are mainly up-milling cuts, so the scale is an assumption about fault strength, not an estimate (Section~\ref{sec:sweep}).

\subsubsection{Features and nodes}
Each window is described by 30 features: log$_{10}$ acceleration rms, microphone level (dB), two spectral non-synchronous indices, ten log band powers each for the accelerometer and the microphone, the kurtosis of both, and the logarithms of spindle speed, radial depth and feed together with the milling direction. Nine of them are aggregated into the sensor nodes of Section~\ref{sec:rca}.

\subsection{Root cause analysis}\label{sec:rca}
\subsubsection{Protocol}
Following the root-cause-ranking protocol of \citet{lee2026brcd}, a healthy dataset $D_\text{obs}$ and $m$ anomalous samples $D_\text{int}$ are given, the failure is treated as an intervention on one root-cause node, and each algorithm returns a ranking of the nodes; we report top-$k$ accuracy of the true root-cause node (chance $k/14$). Cuts are split by identity into 43 training and 30 test cuts (stratified by $a_e$); each point averages 100 random draws of $m$ faulty windows per fault, and all nodes are standardised with $D_\text{obs}$ so that no algorithm benefits from scale differences (Algorithm~\ref{alg:bench}).
\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Benchmark loop for one fault, condition and number of samples $m$.}\label{alg:bench}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE healthy windows $\mathcal H$, faulty windows $\mathcal F_c$ of cause $c$, soft sensors $\{g_j\}$, graph $G$, algorithm $\mathcal A$
\STATE $D_\text{obs}\leftarrow$ nodes of $\mathcal H$ (soft sensors out-of-fold), standardise with its mean and s.d.
\FOR{trial $=1,\dots,100$}
\STATE draw $m$ windows from $\mathcal F_c$ with replacement if needed; compute nodes with $g_j$; standardise $\to D_\text{int}$
\STATE ranking $\leftarrow\mathcal A(D_\text{obs},D_\text{int},G)$
\STATE record whether $\text{node}(c)$ is at rank 1, within 3, within 5
\ENDFOR
\RETURN mean top-1/3/5 over trials and over the five causes
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsubsection{Nodes and graph}
The graph has 14 nodes (Figure~\ref{fig:graph}, Table~\ref{tab:nodes}): five root-cause nodes and nine sensor nodes (log acceleration, microphone level, two non-synchronous indices, three accelerometer and two microphone bands). The 19 directed edges encode physical prior knowledge: each root cause points to the sensor quantities it can affect. Four root-cause nodes are soft sensors, i.e.\ gradient-boosted regressors of the log fault factor trained on training cuts only (out-of-fold predictions for $D_\text{obs}$); the spindle-speed node is the controller-measured deviation.

\subsubsection{The algorithms as implemented}
Table~\ref{tab:algs} compares each algorithm with its description in \citet{lee2026brcd} (Appendix~C of that paper) and states what our code (\texttt{run\_full\_paper\_benchmark.py}) does. \textbf{The implementations are simplified, graph-aware re-implementations, not the authors' code}: all of them score a node by the standardised shift of its mean or of its residual given its parents, under a linear-Gaussian model, and BRCD, RCG, RCD and Smooth Traversal use the known graph. BARO's scoring step and SimpleRCA match their published descriptions. BRCD, RCG, RCD and Smooth Traversal contain constants with no source (0.12, 0.40, 0.70, 0.05). Consequently, our accuracies describe these re-implementations on this benchmark and must not be read as the performance of the original algorithms; the finding that the graph is irrelevant is partly a property of the re-implementations (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).

\subsubsection{Generator shift and real-data transfer}
\paragraph{Generator shift.} Because the soft sensors are trained on labels from the same generator, a \emph{shifted} test multiplies natural frequency, damping, stiffness, force and edge coefficients by random factors in $[0.75,1.33]$, $[0.5,2]$, $[0.6,1.7]$, $[0.6,1.6]$ and $[0.4,2.5]$ per cut, with soft sensors left at their nominal training.
\paragraph{Real-data transfer.} For real wear data, features are expressed relative to the healthy baseline of each tool (the least-worn runs), are invariant to the sign convention of the force axes (absolute mean force) and are evaluated leave-one-machine-out; training modes are simulation only, simulation then fine-tuning on $k$ real samples with an L2 penalty towards the simulated solution, and real only.

\subsubsection{Operating regime and robustness}\label{sec:regime}
Plotting the raw simulator output (Figure~\ref{fig:waveforms}) showed millimetre-scale displacement. At nominal parameters (modal stiffness $3\times10^{6}$~N/m, $f_n{=}1500$~Hz, $\zeta{=}0.03$, $K_t{=}7\times10^{8}$~N/m$^2$) the zero-order limit depth at 9000~rpm is 30.6~mm for $a_e{=}0.0635$~mm and 18.0~mm for $a_e{=}0.3175$~mm, below the real axial depth of 38.1~mm; the time-domain rms displacement is 0.44 and 0.82~mm and the tool leaves the cut ($h_{\min}=0$). \subsubsection{Does real-data transfer degrade when the target dynamics are far from the training machines?}\label{sec:dyn}
Without measured transfer functions for LUH we used a proxy: the mean 21-band force spectrum of the three least-worn runs of each tool. For each of the nine target tools we trained on the tools of the other machines (all six, the three nearest, the three farthest by the proxy). With gradient boosting and raw features at VB $\ge100~\mu$m, AUROC was $0.993$, $0.984$ and $0.976$ for all, nearest and farthest training tools; the Spearman correlation between proxy distance and AUROC was $-0.16$ ($p=0.67$) at VB $\ge100~\mu$m and $-0.34$ ($p=0.36$) at $60~\mu$m (Figure~\ref{fig:dyn}). Only logistic regression on tool-relative features at $60~\mu$m showed a trend ($\rho=-0.62$, $p=0.08$; lowest AUROC $0.25$). With nine targets, a weak proxy and an unknown range of dynamics, this is absence of evidence, not evidence of absence: on these data real-data transfer is sufficient, and whether simulation is needed to reach dynamics outside the training range remains open.

\subsubsection{FRF import}\label{sec:frf}
For a new machine the tool-tip transfer function can be read from hammer-test data (receptance, or accelerance divided by $(j\omega)^2$) and fitted by adding modes at residual peaks with linear residue estimation. On synthetic transfer functions with added noise the shape error is 0.8\% (no noise), 1.7\% (noise 1\% of the peak) and 16\% (3\%, one mode lost); the importer has not been tested on measured FRFs (Figure~\ref{fig:frf}).

\includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{figures/f_frf_fit.png}
\caption{FRF import on a synthetic transfer function with 1\% and 3\% noise: noisy input, true and fitted receptance magnitude.}\label{fig:frf}
\end{figure}

\subsubsection{Negative result: direct calibration of the delay model}\label{sec:neg}
Fitting 15 physical and sensor parameters to real windows with CMA-ES (MMD, later group means over conditions) plateaued at a loss near 3 (squared errors in real standard deviations) and did not converge in 35--40 generations; we stopped by a pre-set rule. Earlier low-MMD fits had wrong dependence on conditions: real microphone level correlates with spindle speed at $+0.85$, the simulation gave $0.00$; simulated acceleration depended on radial depth ($0.69$), the real one did not ($0.06$). A window-length mismatch (4~s real versus 0.18~s simulated) had inflated simulated variance threefold. The data do not constrain the delay model's absolute levels, hence the use of fault \emph{differences}.

Replacing the physical graph by random edges of equal out-degree or by an empty graph changes no algorithm beyond the seed spread (BRCD $0.92\pm0.01$, $0.92\pm0.02$, $0.92\pm0.00$ shared; $0.60\pm0.05$, $0.60\pm0.05$, $0.58\pm0.06$ shifted). Every root-cause node is a graph root, so in our re-implementations the conditional models cannot add information about it and the methods act as marginal shift detectors on the node estimates, consistent with the observation of \citet{lee2026brcd} on BARO and SimpleRCA. Replacing the supervised soft sensors by label-free spectral summaries reduces accuracy to $0.07$--$0.29$ (shared) and $0.07$--$0.37$ (shifted); the simplest method reaches exactly $0.20$, consistent with only the controller-measured speed node remaining informative (not tested directly). Under shift, a threefold larger fault gain lowers accuracy (BRCD $0.60\to0.27$, Smooth Traversal $0.71\to0.28$) because soft sensors trained on nominal parameters mis-estimate larger, distorted effects. The benchmark therefore measures supervised estimation of the root-cause nodes and its sensitivity to generator mismatch, not causal reasoning.

\subsubsection{Feature normalisation for transfer between machines}
Normalising features by each machine's healthy baseline did not raise AUROC on unseen machines (gradient boosting: $0.990$ raw, $0.955$ machine-relative, $0.986$ tool-relative) and increased the wear-estimation error (MAE of VB $9.2$, $17.7$, $11.1~\mu$m). Domain adaptation does not improve on gradient boosting, and tool-relative features make DANN unstable (machine 1: $0.79\pm0.13$; Table~\ref{tab:luhbase}, Figure~\ref{fig:luh}).

\subsubsection{Wear threshold and fine-tuning}
Because heavy wear may be too easy, we repeated the label-free comparison at VB $\ge60$ and $\ge40~\mu$m (thresholds fixed before running; all three are reported). The comparator is the resultant-force rule on the same tool-relative $x,y$ features (Table~\ref{tab:thr}, Figure~\ref{fig:thr}). Averaged over machines, the simulation-only model is higher than the rule at every threshold, but the gain comes from machine~3, where the rule is below chance ($0.42$, $0.40$ and $0.43$) and simulation reaches $0.57$--$0.78$; on machines 1 and 2 the rule is as good as or better than simulation in five of six cases (machine 1 at $60~\mu$m is tied). The evidence for a label-free benefit of simulation is therefore limited to one of three machines, with the sign of the one-feature rule on that machine unexplained.

\section{Conclusion, Limitations and Future Work}\label{sec:concl}
\subsection{Conclusion}
We asked how far simulation can take root cause diagnosis in milling and measured it against real data in three places.
Simulated causal-RCA accuracy depends on how diagnostic nodes are built and falls from 0.80--0.98 to 0.35--0.71 under moderate generator mismatch, while the causal graph has no effect.
The simulated healthy baseline and the signature of a real defective tool remain different from real data, and the nominal simulator operates above its stability limit, which the effect-size gains silently compensate.
On a public wear dataset simulation-only transfer is below a one-feature rule on two machines and brings no fine-tuning benefit, because the task is too easy to show one.
Simulation is necessary in principle for causes without real labels, but its value was not demonstrated on any real task we could test; the contribution of this paper is the decomposition of the simulation gap, the measurements and the open pipeline needed to test it on real multi-cause data.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{No real multi-cause data.} Results are for one tool and material (MSM subset), one defective-tool comparison on a different tool, and force-only wear data (LUH); root-cause ranking has not been validated on real cuts.
\item \textbf{Simulator.} The nominal simulator is above its stability limit at every real cutting condition; fault severity ranges, gains, nominal physical parameters and the causal graph are assumptions; neither standard fault models nor a chipped-flute runout reproduce the defective-tool signature; the healthy baseline is distinguishable from real data (balanced accuracy $0.82$--$0.95$ over the four variants).
\item \textbf{RCA algorithms.} The algorithms are simplified re-implementations with unsourced constants, so the finding on the causal graph does not transfer to the original algorithms; the main causal tables use one split and seed, and only the ablation reports three seeds.
\item \textbf{Data.} The MSM microphone and accelerometer show a machine line near 1.5~kHz that contaminates the 1--3~kHz features; the dynamics-distance test is a weak proxy with nine targets; the FRF importer is tested only on synthetic transfer functions; meta domain generalisation is not implemented as a baseline.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Future work}\label{sec:future}
The decisive experiment needs real multi-cause data. We will collect cuts with designed causes (clamp stiffness, overhang, wear, depth or speed outside the stable range, imbalance, chipped flute) on at least two machines with hammer-test FRFs and force-coefficient calibration cuts, and evaluate by leave-one-machine-out and leave-one-configuration-out with simulation only ($k{=}0$), simulation plus $k$ real samples, real only, and real data from other configurations with and without FRF-anchored simulation (Table~\ref{tab:planned}, Figure~\ref{fig:planned}; protocol in \texttt{paper/DATA\_PROTOCOL.md}). Baselines will include standard classifiers, domain-adversarial transfer \citep{wang2020datwear}, meta domain generalisation \citep{li2023metadg}, rule thresholds, FRF-based stability checks, and faithful implementations of BRCD, RCD and RCG. Decision gates: if the simulator with measured FRFs does not reproduce the measured stability boundary within an agreed tolerance, the model is fixed before more data are collected; if no benefit at small $k$ appears, the contribution is positioned as zero-shot transfer, dataset and gap analysis. Further technical work: a stable-regime parameter set learned from measured FRFs, per-tooth force asymmetry and process damping for the defective-tool signature, calibration and abstention for deployment.

\section{Methodological pitfalls found along the way}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Plain accuracy of a classifier two-sample test with unequal sizes has a chance level above 0.5; use balanced accuracy.
\item Real and simulated features must use windows of identical length; a 20-fold mismatch inflated variance threefold.
\item Out-of-sample residuals are needed to estimate between-cut variation when the regression can interpolate each cut.
\item Distribution matching alone did not identify the fault gain; effect-size matching was used.
\item Nodes trained on the generator's labels make a causal benchmark circular.
\item Force-axis sign conventions differ between machines; a signed mean force put zero-shot transfer below chance.
\item Plotting raw simulator output early would have exposed the stability problem of Section~\ref{sec:regime}; check the operating regime of a simulator against the stability limit before using it.
\end{enumerate}
